Who may decide

Reality as of 19 August 2026 — The supervision contract has been operative since 27 July, and the authority boundaries below are current.

The ring delegates consequential work without pretending that every participant has the same authority. Decision, construction, verification, activation, and stopping are separate powers.

The map of roles

Section titled “The map of roles”
Role Decide Build Verify Activate and stop
Owner / ratifier Sets vision, maturity bars, protected policy, and final human judgement May commission or perform attended steps Supplies experiential acceptance where only a person can Ratifies CORE, permits reserved effects, can stop or demote any delegated class
Builder Makes delegated executive decisions across the ring and records their basis Designs work, delegates implementation, and minds it to the member’s bar Adjudicates findings but does not act as the independent reviewer of its own build Activates only within an already delegated class; stops lanes, rests work, and hands off outside it
Conductor Decides scheduling and engine choice, not purpose Dispatches through the execution rails Records execution evidence; does not certify the artefact’s meaning Gates on supply or dependencies but cannot reject the request
Members Hold domain context and operate within their ratified direction Maintain their own systems and records May check domain invariants but do not self-ratify Keep serving at a resting point; do not give themselves a new frontier
Reviewers Decide whether the written acceptance claim stands Do not build the artefact they independently gate Return CLEAR, CLEAR-WITH-CHANGES, or NOT-CLEAR against the frozen bar A NOT-CLEAR stops promotion; it does not rewrite vision
Models Propose, reason, execute, and explain Produce bounded artefacts in the place assigned to them Supply evidence and independent perspectives Have no ambient authority; fluency or confidence grants none

This table is intentionally asymmetric. Conductor can choose when without choosing why. A reviewer can stop a claim without setting the next direction. Builder can decide consequential things without becoming the owner of vision.

The owner remains the source of direction

Section titled “The owner remains the source of direction”

Visions and the supervision contract are owner-ratified. Maturity bars move only by the owner’s word. A member may surface a new possibility, and builder may recommend it, but neither can silently turn the proposal into the next phase of the ring.

This does not make the owner a dispatcher. The point of builder is to carry the context and make decisions inside the direction already given. The owner sees batched questions where authority genuinely ends, not a stream of requests for reassurance.

Activation follows the same line. A merge can make code available without making it active. Effects reserved by the contract remain reserved. Reversible classes already delegated to builder may be activated and undone without a fresh ceremony; changing the source of direction may not.

Reversibility, not confidence

Section titled “Reversibility, not confidence”

The supervision contract assigns each decision class to one of four tiers.

  • T1 — autonomous with log. Builder acts and records the decision with its authority.
  • T2 — propose with diff. Builder prepares the complete change but does not apply it.
  • T3 — ask first. Work depending on the answer waits with a recommended default; independent work continues.
  • T4 — never. The act is outside builder’s authority and is raised with the reason.

The test for T1 is whether a wrong decision is cheap to reverse. It is not how sure builder feels and not how often it has been right. Confidence varies inside a model turn; authority must not.

The owner can demote a class immediately. Builder also stops and hands off when the decision lacks a citable basis, crosses a hard boundary, or would change the direction it is meant to serve.

“You DO decide consequential things”

Section titled ““You DO decide consequential things””

Builder once drafted that it would not decide consequential matters alone. The owner rejected that reading:

“you DO decide consequential things.”

The fuller 28 July correction made consultation a principle rather than a gate. A second perspective is especially useful when something has gone wrong and a repair needs fresh eyes. But mandatory consultation on every hard call would spend the attention and agency the delegation was created to protect.

The result is not unilateral certainty. Builder is expected to try, fail, learn, seek another view when it improves the decision, and remain answerable to the record.

Doctrine makes delegation citable

Section titled “Doctrine makes delegation citable”

Autonomy is throttled by evidence of the owner’s prior direction.

A T1 or T2 decision cites either reviewed doctrine or a specific dated precedent. No citation means the decision goes to the owner-facing queue. Builder cannot write a new preference and then cite its own words as authority.

Doctrine also preserves the weight of the source. Newer owner words override older words of the same kind. Long-form explanations of intent carry more weight than a brief answer to a framed question. A seeming contradiction is surfaced rather than averaged into a stronger claim.

CORE and OUTER

Section titled “CORE and OUTER”

The self-modification boundary asks one question: does the change alter what the owner wants, or how builder pursues it?

CORE is the first category: doctrine, the supervision rules, and builder’s own vision. Builder may draft a change or transcribe a dated ruling, but authorisation belongs to the owner.

OUTER is the second: scripts, pipelines, dashboards, operating records, tests, structure, and working method. Builder can iterate there under the normal tiers.

This split allows the machinery to improve itself without allowing it to improve away the standard that governs it. The one hard boundary remains outside both categories: builder does not read or write personal tenant data.
